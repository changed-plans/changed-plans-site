THE FAN'S OWN WORDS

Don’t eat the cost.

Fans have a phrase for what happens when plans change and the ticket says “no refunds”: they ate the cost. Most of the time it’s one or two tickets — $100 to $350, gone. It doesn’t have to end that way.

“We couldn’t go and we couldn’t sell, so we just ate the cost.”

— A family that spent $300 on tickets, in a Ticketmaster review. The start time moved 2½ hours; the ticket insurance they’d bought didn’t cover that either.

FOR INDEPENDENT VENUES

Make “plans changed” your customer-service win.

Give patrons a fair, familiar place to pass on tickets while protecting your no-upsell policy and your reputation.

Your fans aren’t complaining to you — they’ve hit a dead end. When plans change, the pain never lands in your inbox. It surfaces as a 1-star review, a dilemma post, a one-line “unable to attend” in a fan group you never see. The pain is never sent to you. That doesn’t mean it isn’t there.

And a strict policy trains them to wait. Nearly 7 in 10 attendees say a restrictive cancellation policy would cause them to register closer to the event (Freeman research, reported by Skift Meetings, Oct 2026). A fan who can’t get out of a ticket learns to buy later — while your caterer, your printer, and your cash flow are planning around people who haven’t committed yet. Give them a fair way out, and they can commit early again.

THE BEHAVIORAL SCAR — FOR VENUES, FESTIVALS & FUNDRAISERS

Late buying isn’t a fan problem. It’s an operations bill.

Fans who learn there’s no fair way out protect themselves the only way they can: they wait. Your operation doesn’t get to wait.

7 in 10

Your deadlines don’t move

Catering counts lock 3–5 business days out and can’t go down. Programs go to press about 4 weeks ahead. Hotel room-block liability crystallizes 14–30 days out. Servers, ushers, security, and bartenders are hired against a forecast — while a fifth to a third of the audience hasn’t bought yet.

What a partner gets

An official fan-to-fan exchange to point patrons to — face-value caps for your events, a simple box-office handoff, and seats filled instead of no-shows. No platform to build, buy, or staff. Changed Plans can’t move your caterer’s deadline; it attacks the reason your fans wait.

Built for your kind of event

Venues & theatres: an answer for the patron at the box-office window. Festivals & catered events: patrons who can commit earlier, with less riding on a guessed guarantee. Galas & fundraisers: no empty tables in front of sponsors, and fewer guests arriving unresearched and unseated.

The re-diagnosis

Late buying gets blamed on the audience — "people just don't commit anymore." But 84% of attendees have already decided to attend at least four weeks before they register. The decision isn't late. The commitment is — and it moved because a no-refund ticket makes waiting the rational choice.

The final-week surge, the catering bought and binned, the deposits bridged out of reserves — that isn't a consumer trend washing over your business. It's your own money leaking, with your own policy holding the wrench.

Venues feel the late-buying problem every season. They've just misdiagnosed it as consumer behavior. This is the re-diagnosis — and the fix starts with giving fans a safe way to commit early: a fair, official way to pass a ticket on when plans change.
